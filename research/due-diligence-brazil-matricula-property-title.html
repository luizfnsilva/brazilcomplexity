Due Diligence in Brazil Starts With the Matrícula: Reading the Title Before You Price the Asset

In Brazil the building you walk through and the property the registry describes are frequently two different objects. The gap between them is not a legal curiosity. It is a line item.

Every Brazilian property has a matrícula: a numbered file at the local Registry of Deeds (Cartório de Registro de Imóveis) that records who owns it, what encumbrances sit on it, and what has been formally declared about the physical asset. It is the only document that establishes ownership. A purchase contract does not. A notarized deed does not, until it is registered.

Foreign investors tend to treat the matrícula as a closing formality handled by counsel. It is better treated as the first document in the underwriting, because what it says — and what it fails to say — changes the price.

Three objects that should be one

There is the physical building. There is the building as the municipality taxes it (the cadastro behind the IPTU bill). And there is the building as the registry describes it. In a clean asset the three agree. In a large share of Brazilian commercial property they do not.

The commonest divergence is built area. An owner expands a floor, encloses a terrace, converts a garage, and never files the averbação that records the new construction. The registry still describes the smaller building. The buyer is acquiring, formally, less than what is standing — and inherits the cost and the municipal process of regularizing the difference, which can require a retroactive building permit, fines, and in some cities a demolition order for the part that cannot be regularized.

For a regulated use such as a clinic, this matters twice. The sanitary authority licenses the building as described in the approved plans. If the plans do not match the registry, and neither matches the structure, the licensing path stalls before it starts.

What sits on the title

The matrícula records encumbrances: mortgages, fiduciary liens, judicial attachments (penhoras), unavailability orders, usufructs, easements. Some are obvious and clear at closing. Others are not. A judicial attachment registered by a creditor of a previous owner does not disappear with the sale. An unavailability order from a labor or tax court blocks the transfer entirely until lifted.

Two categories deserve particular attention because they attach to the property rather than to the person. Condominium charges and IPTU are propter rem obligations: the debt follows the asset, and the new owner answers for it. A building with a delinquent previous owner is cheaper for a reason that only shows up after closing.

The chain and the certificates

Brazilian practice protects a buyer who obtains a standard set of certificates — on the property, on the seller, and on the seller's prior owners within a lookback window — showing no pending claims that could reach the asset. The set varies by state and by the seller's legal form. The practical point for a foreign investor is that the certificate set is a time-stamped photograph: it is valid for a short period, and the closing has to happen inside it.

Where the seller is a company, the inquiry extends to the company's own liabilities, since a fraudulent transfer against creditors can be unwound. Where the seller is an estate, it extends to the probate. Each of these is a routine matter for competent local counsel. None of them is visible from a listing.

Why this is an underwriting variable

The operational cost of a title that needs work is rarely the legal fee. It is the time. Regularizing built area in a slow municipality can take a year. Lifting an attachment requires a court. Closing an estate sale can wait on heirs who do not agree. During that time the asset is carried and not activated.

Two assets with the same listing price and the same physical condition can therefore have materially different costs of ownership. The difference is written in the matrícula, in language that a local reader decodes in minutes and a foreign reader often does not read at all. Pricing the asset without reading it is pricing the wrong object.

Common questions

What is the matrícula of a Brazilian property?

It is the numbered file at the local Registry of Deeds that establishes ownership and records every encumbrance and formal declaration about the property. In Brazil, ownership of real estate transfers only on registration in the matrícula, not on signing a contract or deed.

What is the most common title problem in Brazilian commercial property?

Built area that was never formally recorded. The physical building is larger than the registered one, and the buyer inherits the municipal process, fines and delay of regularizing the difference. For regulated uses, unregistered area can block licensing.

Do unpaid condominium charges and property tax pass to the buyer?

Yes. In Brazil both are obligations that attach to the property rather than to the person, so the new owner answers for arrears left by a previous owner. They are a routine item to verify before pricing.
