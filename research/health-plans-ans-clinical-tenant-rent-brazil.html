The Tenant's Tenant: Health Plans and the Rent Behind the Clinical Rent

A landlord with a clinical tenant in Brazil has a counterparty the lease never names. Most of the tenant's revenue comes from private health plans, which pay on their own cycle, under their own rules, and can decline to pay at all. The rent is downstream of that.

Brazil has a large private health insurance sector — tens of millions of beneficiaries covered by plans regulated by the National Supplementary Health Agency (ANS). Outside the public system, a specialist clinic's patients mostly arrive through these plans. The clinic is paid by the plan, not by the patient, for most of what it does. The landlord is paid by the clinic. The chain has three links, and the lease covers one.

Credentialing is the clinic's license to earn

To be paid by a plan, a clinic must be credentialed (credenciada) by it. Credentialing is granted plan by plan, on the plan's criteria, and it is the plan's decision whether a given city needs another provider in a given specialty. A new clinic in a secondary city can be fully licensed by the sanitary authority and still have no revenue, because the two or three plans that matter in that city have not credentialed it. The timeline for credentialing is not published, is not uniform, and depends on the plan's network strategy in that place.

This is why a lease to a new clinical operator and a lease to an established practice relocating are different credit risks with the same tenant name on the door. The established practice brings its credentials. The new operator has to obtain them, and the landlord is carrying the rent while it does.

The payment cycle and the glosa

Plans pay on their own cycle, commonly weeks after the service, and after review. The review produces glosas: line items the plan declines to pay, for documentation, coding or coverage reasons. Some are reversed on appeal; many are not. A clinic's cash flow is therefore both delayed and discounted relative to its billings, and the discount varies by plan and by specialty.

For the landlord this means the tenant's capacity to pay rent on the first of the month depends on a receivables book whose quality the landlord never sees. A clinic concentrated on one plan has a single point of failure. A clinic whose main plan renegotiates its fee table — which happens — has a revenue shock it did not cause and cannot prevent.

Reading the counterparty that is not in the lease

None of this is hidden. The plans operating in a city, their market share, their credentialing behavior and their reputation for payment are known to every local practitioner. ANS publishes beneficiary and operator data by municipality. A landlord who asks which plans a prospective tenant is credentialed with, how concentrated its revenue is, and how long credentialing took, is asking questions that any local doctor could answer over coffee — and that almost no foreign underwriting asks.

What the premium is actually for

The clinical premium over generic office rent compensates the landlord for building a space the tenant cannot easily leave. The same specificity that holds the tenant also binds the landlord to the tenant's economics. The useful stance is not to avoid the exposure — it is the source of the yield — but to read it: which plans, how concentrated, how established, how long the credentials took. The tenant's tenant is the landlord's counterparty too. It should be underwritten as one.

Common questions

What is credentialing (credenciamento) and why does it matter to a landlord?

It is the plan-by-plan authorization a clinic needs to be paid by a private health plan. A clinic can be fully licensed and still have little revenue until the plans that matter in its city credential it. The timeline is not published and varies by plan and place, and the landlord carries the rent during it.

What is a glosa?

A line item that a health plan declines to pay after reviewing a clinic's billing, for documentation, coding or coverage reasons. Glosas delay and discount the clinic's cash flow relative to its billings, which is what the rent is paid from.

How can a landlord assess this exposure before leasing?

By asking which plans the tenant is credentialed with, how concentrated its revenue is across them, whether it is an established practice or a new operator, and how long its credentials took. ANS publishes operator and beneficiary data by municipality, and local practitioners know the plans' behavior in their city.
